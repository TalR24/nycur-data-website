The core tension

The power paradox

The City Charter calls community boards non-partisan, governmental, public bodies that "consider the needs of the community district" and advise officials.

What it does not give them: any binding decision-making power. Land use votes are advisory. Liquor-license votes are advisory. The budget asks are requests.

So why does any of it matter? Because elected officials, agencies, and the press treat the board's voice as the neighborhood's voice. Influence here is real — it's just informal. The rest of this talk is how to capture it.
NYCuriosity
03

This is the thesis slide. Sit on it.

Boards are advisory only — nothing they pass is binding. New members are often deflated to learn this. Reframe it: advisory bodies that are well-organized and well-connected punch far above their legal weight, because they manufacture the legitimacy electeds and agencies want to borrow.

Concrete example to mention: a unanimous, well-reasoned committee resolution against a bad curb cut gives a council member political cover to fight it. A messy 6-5 vote gives them an excuse to ignore you.

05

Ground-level facts. 59 boards citywide; each has roughly 50 members serving staggered two-year terms, capped at four consecutive terms.

Key power point for later: the Borough President appoints members — that appointment power is the single most direct lever any elected has over a board, because it shapes who's in the room and which way the board leans. File that away.

Community Board 101

What a board actually weighs in on

  • Land use & zoning. Advisory review of rezonings, special permits, and variances under ULURP.
  • Liquor & cannabis licensing. Recommendations to the State Liquor Authority on new and renewing licenses.
  • City budget. Capital & expense priorities for the district, via the District Needs Statement.
  • Service delivery. Sanitation, potholes, noise, and other quality-of-life issues, through the district office.
  • The streetscape. Sidewalk cafés, plaza and street redesigns, and the siting of city facilities.
Nearly all of it is advisory — but the City Planning Commission, agencies, and the State Liquor Authority weigh a board's vote heavily, and a clear position is hard to ignore.
NYCuriosity

New members often don't know the board's actual jurisdiction. Walk these five domains — land use, licensing, budget, service delivery, streetscape.

The throughline: the board rarely decides anything outright, but it's a formal checkpoint in processes that matter (ULURP land-use review, SLA licensing, the budget). A well-argued recommendation shapes what the deciders do.

Tell them: pick the domain you care about — it maps directly to a committee, which we'll get to.

Who actually serves

The seats skew older and wealthier — and that's an opening

Boards are unpaid and meeting-heavy. That quietly screens out renters, parents, students, shift workers, and lower-income residents — so membership tilts toward older, wealthier, longer-tenured residents.

Manhattan board membership, for instance, skews notably older than the borough's adult population.

The flip side: if you bring an underrepresented perspective and you show up consistently, you carry outsized weight in the room. Scarcity is leverage.

Structure · the executive

Who does what on the executive board

  • Chair. Runs meetings, sets agendas, makes committee assignments, speaks for the board. The most powerful seat.
  • Vice Chair(s). Stand in for the Chair and often oversee a portfolio of committees.
  • Secretary. Keeps the minutes and the official record — where your resolutions are memorialized.
  • Treasurer. Oversees the board's small, city-allocated operating budget.
These officers are elected by the board's own members, usually each year. Earning a reputation as a reliable, prepared member is how you eventually win one.
NYCuriosity

Companion to the org chart — now explain the roles. The Chair is the center of gravity: agenda control + committee assignments = real internal power.

Key point that ties to Part 2: officers are elected by the membership, not appointed from outside. So the path to a leadership seat is the same as the path to influence generally — show up, do the work, build trust. Foreshadow the participation lever.

Structure · the office

The District Manager: your most useful ally

  • Paid, full-time staff — hired by the board, not an appointed member.
  • Runs the district office and fields constituent and 311-style complaints.
  • Knows every agency contact and how the bureaucracy actually moves.
  • Convenes the District Service Cabinet — agency reps coordinating local services.
Befriend the District Manager early. They can route your issue to the right agency desk faster than any resolution — and they remember the members who are easy to work with.
NYCuriosity

The District Manager is the most under-appreciated figure on a board. Staff, not a member — so they're constant even as members rotate.

They're the operational hub: complaints, agency relationships, the service cabinet. For a new member, ten minutes with the DM is worth more than an hour reading bylaws.

Ties forward to Lever 2 (stakeholders): the DM is your bridge to agencies and electeds.

The most important distinction

Full board vs. committees

Full board — monthly · all ~50 members · public.

Broad overview of district business. Elected officials attend. Votes are often quick ratifications of committee work, with little new debate.
Committees — usually monthly · small group · public.

Detailed, technical, topic-specific. This is where resolutions get drafted, debated, and shaped. Fewer people, more influence per voice.
NYCuriosity
08

The single most useful thing a new member can internalize. Full board = the ceremony; committees = the workshop.

By the time something hits the full board, the position is usually baked. Real deliberation happened in committee, often weeks earlier, in a room with a handful of people.

Tease the punchline: "If you care about a policy coming before your board, you have to influence it at the committee stage." We'll hit that hard in Part 2.

Committees

A board runs on ~a dozen committees

  • Land Use / Zoning — ULURP, rezonings, variances
  • Transportation — street design, bike & bus lanes, parking
  • SLA / Licensing — liquor & bar license applications
  • Parks & Recreation — open space, park capital projects
  • Landmarks — historic district & landmark applications
  • Cannabis — dispensary licensing & siting
  • Housing — affordability, tenant issues
  • Health & Human Services — clinics, social services
  • Economic Development — small business, jobs
  • Youth & Education — schools, programs
  • Environment / Sanitation — waste, resiliency
  • Executive — runs the board itself
Exact committees vary by board. Each reviews the applications and issues in its domain and drafts the resolutions the full board votes on. Pick the one that matches what you care about.

Where a decision is really made

The underrated on-ramp

You don't need an appointment to have influence: be a public member

  • Most boards let non-appointed residents join committees as public members.
  • You join the discussion fully — research, debate, draft language.
  • In some boards, public members vote on committee resolutions.
  • Far less time commitment than a full board seat.
It's the highest-leverage, lowest-barrier way in: you operate at the committee stage — the exact place where decisions are shaped — without waiting on a Borough President's appointment.
NYCuriosity
10

This is the slide that surprises people, so emphasize it. Public membership = join a committee without a formal appointment.

Because the action is in committee, a committed public member can be more influential than an absentee appointed member. And in boards where public members vote on committee resolutions, you're directly shaping what goes to the full board.

Great fit for anyone in the room who is time-constrained or not yet appointed — pick one committee, go deep.

Two tactical notes: (1) the public session is your two minutes — come with a tight, specific ask, not a ramble. (2) The real networking happens at adjournment — that's when you catch the council member's staffer or the agency rep in the hallway. Don't leave early.

The rules of the game

Robert's Rules of Order, without the fear

Every board runs on parliamentary procedure. It exists to do one thing: let a group make decisions fairly — majority rules, while the minority keeps its rights.

  • Quorum: a majority of appointed members must be present to do business.
  • Through the chair: you're recognized by the Chair before you speak.
  • On the record: every vote is public and recorded.
Don't memorize the whole book. Learn four motions (next slide) and you can participate confidently in 95% of what happens.
NYCuriosity
12

Robert's Rules intimidates newcomers and that intimidation suppresses participation — which is itself a loss of influence. Defang it.

The purpose in one line: orderly majority rule that still protects the minority's right to be heard. Quorum, recognition by the chair, public recorded votes. That's the spine.

Reassure them: you don't need to be a parliamentarian. Master a handful of motions and you're fine.

NYCuriosity
13

These four cover the vast majority of floor action. Give a quick live read of each with the example phrasing.

Amend is the sleeper power tool — it's how you change an outcome without killing a motion outright. Point of order is your safeguard when a chair is steamrolling procedure (e.g., voting without quorum).

Encourage them to actually say "I move…" — many new members never make a motion and so never set the agenda.

Voting & debate

How votes are counted, and how to be heard

The vote
  • To pass: more yes than no + abstentions combined.
  • No proxies, no absentee ballots, no phone votes. Be in the room.
  • Open ballot — each member's vote is public record.
The etiquette
  • Address the Chair, or others through the Chair.
  • Let others speak before you speak again.
  • A good chair makes sure all sides are heard.
NYCuriosity
14

Two columns: mechanics and manners.

Counting quirk worth flagging: abstentions effectively count as no — passage needs yes > (no + abstentions). So abstaining is not neutral; it raises the bar. Decide and vote.

No proxies / absentee / phone — attendance is everything, which ties back to influence being a function of showing up.

Etiquette isn't just politeness; "let others speak before you speak again" is how you avoid becoming the member everyone tunes out.

How to be an effective member

Three levers of influence, the tools that matter most, and where to spend your limited time.

The model

Influence runs on three levers

Lever 1

Coalitions

Build relationships with fellow members. Know their leanings, ally with the aligned, and bridge to the rest.

Lever 2

Stakeholders

Build genuine working relationships with elected officials, their staff, and agency leads — the people with budgets and decisions.

Lever 3

Participation

Show up, speak up, and build a track record. Credibility compounds over time.

NYCuriosity
16

Frame the whole playbook with this triad — we'll take one slide on each. None of them is in the City Charter. All three are about people and persistence, not formal authority.

Tell them: you don't have to be great at all three. Most effective members are strongest on one. But you should be deliberate about which.

Lever 1 · Coalitions

Count votes before the meeting, not during it

  • Map the room. Learn each member's priorities and political leanings.
  • Lock in your allies. Coordinate with members who already agree — show up together.
  • Build bridges. Find the one thing a skeptic cares about and meet them there.
  • Trade, don't fight. Support their resolution this month; they'll remember yours next month.
A resolution's fate is usually decided in hallway conversations and emails before the vote — not in the debate. Effective members do the work between meetings.
NYCuriosity
17

Coalition-building is just retail politics inside a 50-person body. Know who wants what.

The reciprocity point lands well: boards are repeat games. The member who helps others get their wins banks goodwill they can spend later. The lone wolf who's right but allies with no one loses.

Concrete habit: before a contentious vote, you should roughly know the count already. Surprises mean you didn't do the pre-work.

Lever 2 · Stakeholders

The people with budgets and decisions outside the board

  • Council member staff. They control discretionary funding for local projects — and they listen to engaged board members.
  • Agency liaisons (DOT, Parks, DSNY). They implement; a working relationship moves your issue up the queue.
  • Borough President's office. Appointments, capital funds, and convening power.
  • The District Manager. Your in-house connector to all of the above.
A lot of what a board "achieves" happens outside formal procedure — a call to the right staffer, a project added to a capital list. Relationships are the currency.
NYCuriosity
18

The board's formal power is thin, but it sits next to people with real power. Effective members are brokers to those people.

Discretionary funding is the headline: City Council members allocate pots of money to local groups and capital projects. A board member with a relationship and a concrete ask can route real dollars to a park, a school, a street fix.

Name the District Manager again — they know every agency contact. Befriend the DM early.

Lever 3 · Participation

Credibility compounds

  • Speak up — articulate, prepared, specific. A clear two-minute case beats a vague ten.
  • Create a record. Put your position on paper; let it be cited.
  • Be the reliable one. Do the reading, hit the meetings, follow through.
  • Play the long game. Many City Council members started as board members.
The member everyone trusts to have done the homework gets deferred to — on appointments, on chairmanships, on which resolutions move. Reputation is power you bank meeting by meeting.
NYCuriosity
19

Participation seems obvious but it's where most members underperform — they attend and stay silent, or speak unprepared.

The compounding point: every prepared, reasonable intervention builds a reputation. Over a term or two, that reputation is what gets you a committee chair, a seat at the table with electeds, deference on your issues.

The career note (boards as a launchpad for council members) motivates the ambitious folks in the room without being cynical about it.

The highest-leverage move

If you remember one thing tonight, remember this

"If you care about a policy that's coming before a community board, you have to influence that policy when it's at the committee stage."

Everything else — coalitions, stakeholders, participation — pays off most when you apply it early, in committee, before positions harden.

The anchor quote of the whole talk. Say it slowly, let it sit.

Connect it to the funnel from slide 9: leverage lives upstream. The members who consistently win are the ones in the committee room shaping the first draft — not the ones objecting at the full board after the fact.

Soft power, concentrated

Want more leverage? Chair a committee

  • You set the committee agenda — what gets heard and when.
  • You shape which resolutions get drafted and how they're worded.
  • You become the board's point of contact for agencies and applicants in your domain.
  • You build the relationships and record that lead to bigger roles.
Committee chairs hold outsized soft power: committees are where policy is actually debated and resolutions are written. Volunteer for one as soon as you're ready.
NYCuriosity
21

Natural next step after "the committee is everything": run one.

Chairs control agendas and drafting — the two points of maximum leverage. It's also the most reliable path to building the kind of record that earns deference board-wide.

Encourage the engaged folks: express interest to your board chair. Chairs are often desperate for members willing to do the work.

The Swiss Army knife

When a board passes a resolution

  • Land-use actions — support, oppose, or condition a ULURP application.
  • Licensing — recommend for or against a liquor or cannabis license.
  • Legislation — take a position on a city or state bill.
  • Budget priorities — what the district needs funded (feeds the DNS).
  • Memorializing agreements — pin down what an applicant promised.
A resolution is the board's formal voice — one position, on the record. No legal force, but it's the document electeds, agencies, and reporters actually quote.
NYCuriosity

Before dissecting a resolution's structure, ground it: what are these things even for? Five common triggers.

The recurring theme — advisory but quotable. A resolution converts a messy room of opinions into a single citable position. That's its power: it gives an ally downtown something concrete to point to.

The Swiss Army knife

Anatomy of a resolution

Title
One sentence: what this resolution is about.
Whereas… (×N)
The context, background, and reasoning — your argument and the record of agreements.
Therefore, be it resolved
The specific action(s) the board is asking for.

Resolutions are the board's primary product — its formal position on a license, a land-use action, a budget ask, a policy.

They carry no legal force, but a clear, well-reasoned resolution is what an ally downtown actually quotes.

NYCuriosity
22

Teach the three-part structure so anyone in the room could draft one: Title, Whereas clauses (the why), and the Resolved (the ask).

Emphasize the Resolved must be specific and actionable — "the board urges DOT to install a protected bike lane on X between Y and Z," not "the board supports safer streets." Vague asks get ignored; specific asks get cited.

A resolution, annotated

What a good one looks like

Title
"Resolution supporting a protected bike lane on Example Ave between 1st & 14th St."
Whereas… (×N)
"…the corridor saw 23 reported injuries over three years; and Whereas DOT has identified Example Ave as a Vision Zero priority…"
Be it resolved
"…that the board urges DOT to install a parking-protected bike lane on Example Ave from 1st to 14th St by the end of FY2027."
  • Title — scannable; a reader knows the ask in one line.
  • Whereas — real numbers and named sources, not adjectives.
  • Resolved — names the agency, location, and deadline. Specific enough to act on.
NYCuriosity

Make it tangible with a worked example — a street-safety resolution. Read the three parts aloud and contrast good vs. vague.

Drive the lesson on the Resolved clause: "urges DOT to install a protected lane on Example Ave from 1st to 14th by end of FY2027" is something an agency and a council member can act on and be held to. "Supports safer streets" is not. Specificity is what makes a resolution useful downtown.

This example is illustrative — swap in a real one from your board if you have a good case.

Make it persuasive

Write the resolution they'll actually quote

  • Lead with evidence. Put real data and specifics in the Whereas clauses.
  • Memorialize agreements. Pin down exactly what an applicant promised.
  • Make the ask concrete. Name the agency, the location, the action.
  • Draft it in committee. That's where wording is set — own the pen.
Most resolution work happens in committee; the full board often votes with little discussion. Whoever drafts, wins. And in some boards, public members vote on these — outsized influence for low barrier.
NYCuriosity
23

"Whoever drafts, wins" is the line to land. The drafter frames the issue, picks the evidence, and writes the ask. Amendments nibble at the edges; the frame usually survives.

Practical: volunteer to write the first draft. Most members won't, so the offer is almost always accepted — and it hands you the most leverage in the room.

Repeat the public-member kicker: in boards where they vote on committee resolutions, a public member who drafts is punching way above their formal status.

The underappreciated power tool

The District Needs Statement has teeth

Once a year, the board compiles its district's priorities, funding requests, and needs into a formal statement to the city.

Why it's different from an ordinary resolution: city agencies must respond to community board budget requests in the city's annual budget. It plugs the board directly into the money.

Most members ignore it. That's the opportunity — shape your committee's section and you put your priority in front of agencies that are obligated to answer.
NYCuriosity
24

The DNS (sometimes "Statement of District Needs / Budget Requests") is the board's annual budget ask, and it's the rare CB instrument that triggers a required response from agencies in the city budget process.

The strategic gift: it's under-loved, so the marginal effort of writing a strong, specific budget request is low and the payoff is real. Tie it to committees — each committee feeds its section.

Stay clean

The guardrails: conflicts of interest

  • Disclose any conflict of interest, on the record.
  • Recuse & abstain from votes that touch your private interest.
  • Never use your board title or city resources for political or personal gain.
Members must follow NYC Charter Chapter 68 and Conflicts of Interest Board (COIB) guidance. One credibility scandal undoes years of reputation — the very thing your influence is built on.
NYCuriosity
26

Brief but necessary. Chapter 68 + COIB govern board members like any public servant. The three habits: disclose, recuse, don't trade on the title.

Tie it back to the playbook: your entire influence rests on credibility (Lever 3). A conflicts problem is the fastest way to vaporize it. When in doubt, disclose and step back — it costs you one vote and protects everything else.

Avoid these

What ineffective members do

  • Only show up to the full board, after decisions are made.
  • Speak long and unprepared; never make a motion.
  • Treat opponents as enemies — and build no coalition.
  • Ignore the District Manager, electeds, and agency staff.
  • Skip the District Needs Statement entirely.
  • Burn out by trying to fight every issue instead of owning one.
NYCuriosity
27

The inverse of the playbook, which makes it memorable. Each bullet maps to something we covered.

The last one is the most common and most fixable: new members try to be everywhere, burn out, and quit. Pick one committee, one or two issues, go deep. Depth beats breadth on a board.

Your quick-start

The effective-member checklist

  • Pick one committee and attend every meeting.
  • Learn the committee calendar; engage issues early.
  • Volunteer to draft a resolution.
  • Make a clean main motion at your next meeting.
  • Introduce yourself to the District Manager & a council staffer.
  • Map your board's allies and skeptics.
  • Put one priority in the District Needs Statement (June).
  • Stay clean — disclose, recuse, follow COIB.
