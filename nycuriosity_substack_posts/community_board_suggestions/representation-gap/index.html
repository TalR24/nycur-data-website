Boards Speak for Their Neighborhoods. They Don't Yet Look Like Them.

The City Comptroller's October 2024 audit of board appointments compared members' self-reported demographics with Census data. Its starkest finding: Hispanic and Latino residents are underrepresented on community boards in every borough, by 12 points in Brooklyn and by 36 in the Bronx, where they are a majority of the population. An efficient meeting of an unrepresentative board is still an unrepresentative voice.

The gap is not limited to one group. The same audit found Asian residents underrepresented in Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island, younger residents underrepresented everywhere, and no NYCHA residents at all on any Staten Island board. It also found all five borough presidents filed their Charter-required demographic reports late and incomplete.

Why it belongs in an efficiency article: the 2018 Charter amendment told borough presidents to seek out members of diverse backgrounds precisely because a board's advisory power rests on its claim to speak for the district. Representation and effectiveness are the same project.

Source: Office of the New York City Comptroller, "Audit Report on the Five Borough Presidents' Offices' Compliance with the New York City Charter Requirements for Community Board Member Appointments" (SR23-060A et al., October 11, 2024), Appendices 1 through 5, Table I for each borough.

Caveats: Member demographics are voluntarily self-disclosed on board applications; disclosure rates ranged from 82.8% (Bronx) to 98.2% (Staten Island). Borough president application forms use categories that differ from the Census (for example, some members who identify in part as Hispanic/Latino appear under "two or more races"), so the member shares are approximate. The direction of the gap is consistent across every borough and category mapping.
