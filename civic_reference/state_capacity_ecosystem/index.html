The State Capacity Ecosystem

A directory of 300+ organizations working to make American government more effective — and a network view of who is likely to collaborate with whom.

"State capacity" is a clunky term for a simple idea: the ability of government to actually deliver on what it promises — paving streets, processing benefits, building housing, running elections. The orgs in this database approach that problem from very different angles. Research shops diagnose dysfunction. Advocacy groups push for reform. GovTech vendors and digital services teams build the systems. Philanthropies and investors fund the work. Fellowships route talent into government. The point of this tool is to make the shape of that ecosystem legible: who exists, what they do, and which orgs cluster together by mission, funding, and focus.

What gets included

An org is added when it (a) actively works on the effectiveness, capacity, or legitimacy of American government, (b) is currently operating, and (c) has a clearly identifiable mission. The bar is wider than "civic tech" — it covers research shops, advocacy groups, philanthropies, fellowships, GovTech vendors, investors, ecosystem-builders, capacity-building organizations, community groups, and government bodies that themselves do the work of building state capacity.

The bar excludes: defunct organizations, individual consultancies without a public mission, and orgs whose work is not centered on government effectiveness even if government is one customer among many. Edge calls are made by the curator.

Problem statements

Every org carries one or more problem topic tags (37 fine-grained issues) grouped under a problem area (8 broader buckets like Service Delivery, Procurement & Operations, Technology & Data, Talent & Hiring, Capacity). Topics cover both operational challenges (Procurement Reform, Legacy Systems, Hiring Architecture) and policy verticals (Housing & Land Use, Healthcare Access, Broadband, Criminal Justice, Tax & Revenue).

Problem statements are the highest-signal tag in the database — they're the strongest input to the affinity score because they encode what an org is actually trying to fix, independent of how it's structured or who funds it. Two orgs in different segments sharing problem statements often is more informative than two orgs in the same segment with no shared problems.
