Twelve Areas of Study Became Five Ballot Questions

COGE's preliminary report, published in June 2026, laid out twelve areas where the City Charter slows government down. Its final report, adopted unanimously on July 23, turned those into five ballot questions. This is where each one landed, and it doubles as a map of the reform work the Charter could not reach.

The pattern is consistent. Where the Charter itself creates a delay, such as a mandatory hearing or a duplicated approval, COGE proposed to strike it. Where the delay comes from staffing, State law, or agency practice, COGE documented it and moved on. That is a defensible reading of what a Charter Revision Commission is for, and it explains why technology, civil service, and payment timeliness are absent from the ballot.

It also hands the next actors a to-do list. Civil service reform runs through Albany. Reporting requirements and the surety bond can be fixed by local law. Franchise and concession relief is Franchise and Concession Review Committee rulemaking. Building code authority and technology procurement are waiting for a future commission that takes them up directly.

How the areas are defined: the twelve rows correspond to the twelve sub-sections of the preliminary report's four chapters. Two of them, street infrastructure and activating City property, were combined with office leasing into a single ballot question, and permitting and waterfront permitting were combined into another, which is how twelve areas produce five questions.

Procurement is split deliberately. Ballot Question 2 carries the Charter-level procurement changes. The substantive payment reforms, including advance payments, the discretionary grant pilot, and the umbrella agreement model, appear in the final report's Appendix as administrative commitments.

Sources: COGE Preliminary Report (June 2026); COGE Adopted Final Report (July 23, 2026); NYC Charter Revision Commission.
