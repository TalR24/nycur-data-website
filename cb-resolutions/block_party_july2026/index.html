Why are Community Boards important?

local

Community boards produce more structured civic data than almost any other level of government.

Community boards discuss policies, initiatives, priorities which impact our day to day lives. They act as the community’s voice when brokering conversations with state and local governments, non profit agencies and other organizations. PART ONE

What Are NYC Community Boards
and Why Do They Matter?

The Local Layer of NYC Government Most People Don’t Know Exists

Community boards are the ground floor of New York City politics—and most New Yorkers have never heard of them.

· 59 community boards across NYC’s 5 boroughs

· Established by the NYC Charter

· Represent a single community district — neighborhood scale

· Members are uncompensated volunteers appointed by their Borough President to serve two-year terms

· Charter Mandate: “consider the needs of the community district and advise elected officials”

The key fact:

Community boards have NO formal decision-making power.

Their role is advisory.

So why do they matter? →

Soft Power — Why “Advisory” Doesn’t Mean Toothless

▶

Resolutions on the record

Every agency and elected official sees when CB formally opposes their project. Ignoring a unanimous board vote can have political costs.

▶

District Needs Statement (DNS)

Annual priorities document filed by each CB. City agencies must respond to DNS requests in budget planning — direct budget influence.

▶

Required ULURP referrals, SLA & sidewalk cafe applications

For major land use decisions (rezonings, new developments), the city is legally required to refer applications to the CB. Recommendations become public record.

▶

Political pipeline

Many City Council members began as CB members. Relationships here open doors in elected offices and city agencies.

How Community Boards Are Structured

Who’s on the board:

· Up to 50 volunteer members

· 2-year terms (max 4 consecutive)

· No salary, no formal qualifications

· Only requirement: Must live, work, or have significant interest in the district

Leadership & staff:

· Health · Youth & Education

What Is a Resolution?

Resolutions are official, written, public positions on a local issue.

Standard resolution format:

Two Kinds of Community Board Meetings

Full Board Meetings

Monthly · Open to the public

· Public comment period (~2 min per person)

· Elected officials give reports

· Committees report resolutions

· Full Board votes on resolutions

Committee Meetings

(Usually) Monthly · (Usually) Open to the public

· Agencies present project details

· Resolutions drafted and debated by committees

· Where advocacy is most effective

· Where the real work happens

Advocate insight: By the time a resolution reaches the full board, the debate is mostly over. Show up to committee meetings.

Quick recap of the March state: everything you just saw ran on one board, MCB3, with hand-run scripts and a parser that knew exactly one document format. The closing slide of the original talk asked how we would ever generalize it. This new section is a progress report on exactly that question.

Step One: Ship a Real Pipeline, Not More Scripts

The procedural scripts were rewritten as an object-oriented pipeline.

-> Board-specific logic moved out of the code and into per-board config files.

One domain object

A ResolutionDocument flows through every stage: discover → fetch → extract → parse → embed → persist.

Pydantic validators guard each step.

Config-driven boards

Each board is a YAML file: source type, URL patterns, parser options, filters. Adding a board means writing config, not forking code.

All 12 Manhattan boards currently have one.

A real CLI + tests

extract · reextract · audit · report · export-csv · migrate, each a command.

131 tests cover parsers, sources, and pipeline fixes.

The big decision: stop patching scripts and build the pipeline the scaffolding always implied. One domain object with a validated lifecycle, board behavior expressed as YAML config, and a CLI so every operation is repeatable. 131 tests mean a parser change on one board cannot silently break another.

Every Board Publishes Differently, So Discovery Is Pluggable

Six source adapters feed one shared pipeline. The board’s YAML names its adapter; everything downstream is identical.

NYCGovAssetsSource  MCB3: bundled meeting-minute PDFs on nyc.gov, four URL eras back to 2002

WordPressIndexSource  MCB2, 4, 9, 10: scrapes the boards’ WordPress index pages for document links

GoogleDriveFolderSource  MCB7: lists a public Google Drive folder, 380 PDFs back to 2000

WaybackCdxSource  MCB8: queries the Internet Archive’s CDX index when a board’s own site can’t be crawled

AirtableSharedViewSource  MCB5, 6, 11, 12: harvests public share views through the signed downloadCsv endpoint, no API key needed

AirtableInterfacePageSource  MCB1: headless Chromium intercepts the interface page’s own data call; full resolution text arrives in-table

The core insight of the scale-up: boards differ most in where their documents live, not what the documents say. So discovery became a pluggable adapter. Six adapters now cover every hosting pattern we have met across Manhattan: city-hosted PDFs, WordPress sites, a Google Drive folder, the Internet Archive, and two flavors of Airtable: share views harvested without an API key, and an interface page read by a headless browser.

Some of It Was Pure Detective Work

MCB7: the Drive folder in the JS bundle

CB7 rebuilt its site as a React app with no document index. The public Google Drive folder ID was sitting in the compiled JavaScript: 380 dated PDFs back to 2000.

Airtable opened five boards, no API key needed

The public share page carries a server-signed URL; the view’s downloadCsv endpoint then signs every attachment. MCB5, 6, 11, and 12 came in this way; MCB5 went from 40 resolutions to 3,031, a 76× gain.

MCB1: intercepting the page’s own API call

MCB1’s share is an interface page with no CSV export. Headless Chromium captures the page’s own readForSharedPages call and rewrites it from msgpack to JSON: 5,070 documents, zero PDF downloads, about 30 seconds.

Extraction Is Now Hybrid: Regex First, Claude When It Matters

1 · Regex parser

Ported from the original extractor. Handles the standard formats at zero token cost.

2 · Quality gates

Heuristics score every parse; missing clauses, suspicious titles, and empty bodies trigger escalation.

3 · Claude fallback

Documents that fail the gates go to Claude Sonnet for structured extraction instead.

Trust, but verify: every run also re-parses a deterministic 5% sample with both methods and compares them, so format drift on any board surfaces as a diff, not as silent data corruption.

The March pipeline was pure regex, which fails silently when a board changes its format. The rewrite made extraction hybrid: regex handles the standard cases for free, quality heuristics catch the failures, and Claude extracts the hard documents. The 5% dual-parse sample is the insurance policy: drift shows up as a comparison diff before it becomes bad data.

Teaching One Parser Eleven Dialects

Each new board broke assumptions MCB3 never tested.

The fix loop: dump → audit → fix, run five to seven rounds per board group:

Envelope & letterhead stripping  CB letters wrap resolutions in addresses, salutations, signature closings, and page footers; letterhead leaking into text fell 83% → 1% on MCB2

Bundle splitting  One PDF often holds many resolutions; numbered-item and tally-terminated splitting recovered them as separate records

Prose vote tallies  “12 in favor, 2 opposed” written as sentences now parses into structured counts

Noise-title filters  Rows titled with bare dates, vote tallies, or website page chrome are dropped board-wide; zero-clause rows on MCB2 fell 76% → 8%

True meeting counts  Meetings now key on board + date, so one-PDF-per-resolution boards stopped reporting one “meeting” per document

Every change lands in a per-board scorecard, so drift is a ledger entry; residual pollution on the Airtable boards is at or under one row per board.

This is where most of the real hours went. MCB3 trained us on one dialect of the resolution format; the other boards each speak their own. The loop that worked: dump the board, audit the output, fix the parser, five to seven rounds per board group. The metrics here are from that ledger: letterhead contamination down from 83% of rows to 1%, empty-body rows from 76% to 8%, and residual pollution at or under one row per board on the Airtable boards.

Every Resolution Now Carries More

The March schema stored a title, clauses, and a vote. New extractions add:

Verbatim text + provenance

The exact source text of every clause, plus which document and parser version produced each record.

Three-field summaries

Claude Haiku writes a structured title / context / action summary per resolution (the exact shape blockparty.studio renders).

Bigger embeddings

Chunk vectors upgraded to text-embedding-3-large (3,072 dimensions) for sharper semantic search across boards.

Structured votes

Yes / no / abstain / present-not-voting counts and outcome, parsed even from prose tallies in letter formats.

Summaries + provenance mean a reader can trust the gist and still check the source.

The record itself got richer. Where March gave us a title, clauses, and a vote, each resolution now keeps its verbatim source text with provenance, a structured three-field summary written by Claude Haiku in exactly the shape the Block Party site displays, full vote breakdowns, and 3,072-dimension embeddings for semantic search.

The Plumbing Became Real Infrastructure

Storage

·  Supabase Postgres + pgvector is the source of truth
·  Three tables: meetings, resolutions, text chunks
·  Schema changes ship as numbered migrations
·  Re-runs preserve existing record UUIDs, so links never break

Automation

·  Per-board backfills in GitHub Actions: idempotent upserts, weekly cron for deltas
·  Dry-run mode for safe rehearsal
·  Content-addressed PDF cache makes re-extraction cheap
·  Audit + report commands catch regressions between runs

Battle scars included: the cron shook out real-world bugs: nyc.gov blocking bot user-agents, token-limit truncation on 19-resolution meetings, streaming requirements on long extractions. Each fix is now a regression test.

None of this is glamorous, and all of it is the difference between a demo and a system. Supabase with pgvector is the single source of truth, schema changes are migrations, UUIDs survive re-runs so nothing downstream breaks, and GitHub Actions runs each board’s backfill with idempotent upserts while a weekly cron picks up deltas. The bugs the cron surfaced are all regression tests now.

What Made It Hard (and What We Learned)

The web decays.  Four URL eras on one board, a site rebuilt into a React app, Cloudflare walls, dead domains. Public records rot in place.

Green doesn’t mean done.  On the first wet run, every chunk insert failed on a legacy NOT NULL column while the workflow reported success; embeddings were paid for and discarded. Verification now counts actual rows.

A trimmer ate the long letters.  MCB12’s letters repeat the addressee at every page break; the trimmer read it as a new letter and cut every multi-page resolution to one page. The tell: bodies ending mid-clause.

A heuristic ate real titles.  The chair-signature pattern matched every address-led Midtown title and blanked 297 MCB5 titles. Human-curated Airtable titles are never overridden now.

Regex fails silently on OCR text.  A word-boundary anchor finds nothing in space-squished text like “InFavor0Opposed”; no boundary exists between word characters. Every pattern change is re-measured against the corpus.

If you take one meta-lesson from this section: the failure mode of civic data pipelines is silence. A green run that discarded every embedding, a trimmer that halved documents, a heuristic that blanked 297 real titles, a regex that matched nothing: none of them threw an error. What works is verification that counts actual rows and an audit loop that re-measures every change against the corpus.
