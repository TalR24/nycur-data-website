Ninety-Four Years at the Subway's Crossroads

West 4th Street–Washington Square opened in 1932 with an entire lower level built for a line that would not exist for another eight years. This timeline traces the station from that bet on the future through 27 years without express tracks, a failed first generation of elevators, and the renewal completed in May 2026.

West 4th Street–Washington Square, 1932–2026
Every major event in the station's construction, expansion, decline, and renewal.
Building the crossroads · 1932–1940
1932 Sept 10
The upper level opens with the city-built IND Eighth Avenue Line
West 4th Street is designed as the express hub where the Independent Subway System's two Manhattan trunk lines will meet: four island platforms and eight tracks on two levels, joined by a full-length mezzanine. The Sixth Avenue trunk does not exist yet, but its lower level is built anyway.
WHY "WEST"? IND planners added "West" to avoid confusion with South 4th Street, a planned Brooklyn hub of the never-built IND Second System. The station is named for a ghost. NYU asked for purple tiles during construction and was refused: tile color was a navigation system, and West 4th's green bands marked an express stop.
1936 Jan 1
The lower level carries its first trains, four years before its own line opens
E trains begin running through the lower level's southern tracks to the new Houston–Essex Street Line. The waiting shell turns out to be usable infrastructure ahead of schedule.
1940 Dec 15
The Sixth Avenue Line opens and the two-trunk design is finally in service
Eight years after the station opened, the line its lower level was built for arrives. West 4th Street becomes the only station where the IND's two Manhattan trunk lines meet, a distinction it still holds.
Completing the design · 1961–1967
1961 Apr 19
Ground breaks on the $22M express tracks the lower level had waited on since 1940
The Sixth Avenue Line between West 4th and 34th Street opened with no express tracks. Crews begin threading two new tracks through one of the most congested underground corridors in the city, beneath the existing local tracks and around the PATH tubes.
1967 Nov 26
B and D expresses arrive via the Chrystie Street Connection
After 27 years as a local-only line through the station's lower level, Sixth Avenue express service begins. The station's full eight-track design is finally used as drawn.
Decades of wear · 1970–2010
1970
Escalators finally connect the two levels
Two up-only escalators link the Sixth Avenue platforms to the Eighth Avenue level, five years after the Manhattan Borough President first requested funding. They get a $4.3M renovation in 1998.
1978–1990
The JFK Express stops at the lower level
The premium-fare airport train gives West 4th Street a direct link to JFK for twelve years before the service is discontinued.
2004 July
The station is listed on the National Register of Historic Places
The registration recognizes the station as a signature work of the IND, the subway system New York City built itself.
2005 April
Three elevators make the station ADA-accessible, then fail almost immediately
In 2005 alone the new elevators are out of service a cumulative nine months; one stays closed 134 straight days. Riders file 57 elevator complaints, including seven trapped-rider incidents, and disability-rights groups sue the MTA over systemwide elevator failures.
2010
The M joins via the Williamsburg Bridge
Service changes route the M through the Chrystie Street Connection to Sixth Avenue, bringing the station's roster to its current seven services: A, C, E, B, D, F, M.
Renewal · 2025–2026
2025 Jul 31
Six new escalators enter service
A design-build contract replaces the station's escalators (alongside Sutphin Blvd–Archer Av in Queens), with new LED lighting throughout.
2026 May 13
The last of three replacement elevators enters service, three weeks early
Under the MTA's 19 Elevators Project, West 4th Street gets two new platform-to-mezzanine elevators and a new street elevator at West 3rd and Sixth Avenue with a glass canopy. The station's second generation of accessibility hardware is complete.
