How Long a Rule Takes

Laws and rules are often enacted with little advance testing, so edge cases and unintended consequences surface only after they cause harm. Part of the reason is speed: writing a federal rule is slow. The chart below shows how slow, from the fastest cases to the longest.

Why it matters: Across the rules GAO studied, writing one took about four years on average, ranging from one year to nearly fourteen. By the time a rule is final, the problem it was meant to fix has often changed. Policies enacted without testing reveal their flaws only in the field.

Where AI helps: Stress-testing a draft against thousands of scenarios, red-teaming it for loopholes and excluded groups, and iterating quickly. As Jennifer Pahlka argues, if a small team can map a regulatory regime in a week, the standard for due diligence in lawmaking starts to move. Simulations supplement real-world piloting, they do not replace it.

Examples to follow

  • Edge-case stress-testing — running a draft policy against thousands of hypothetical situations before it is enacted.
  • Red-teaming drafts — prompting a model to find the loopholes, perverse incentives, and groups a draft accidentally excludes.
  • Rapid drafting and iteration — letting legislative staff revise text and immediately re-test it.
  • Regulatory regime mapping — charting how a proposed rule interacts with everything already on the books, in days rather than months.

Rulemaking duration: Across 16 case-study rules, GAO found completion took about four years on average, ranging from roughly one year to nearly fourteen. Source: GAO-09-205, "Federal Rulemaking" (2009).

Note: "Fastest" and "Slowest" are the ends of GAO's observed range, not strict minimums or maximums across all federal rules. Other estimates (CRS) put typical administrative rulemaking at one to two years.

AI timeline: The "map a regulatory regime in a week" comparison is an illustration from Jennifer Pahlka's writing on government reform, not a measured benchmark.
